I just finished *Man's Search for Meaning* by Viktor Frankl, and it's the kind of book I keep thinking about days after closing it.

Frankl was a psychiatrist who survived the concentration camps, and the first half of the book is his account of that. But it isn't really a book about suffering. It's a book about why some people kept going when, by any rational measure, there was nothing left to keep going for. His answer is simple and hard to shake: the ones who survived tended to have something to live *for*, whether a person, a piece of unfinished work, or a reason. Meaning, not comfort, was what carried them.

That idea reorganised something for me. I spend my days turning messy data into answers, and there's a version of that mindset that quietly optimises your whole life for comfort and efficiency: fewer problems, smoother days, less friction. Frankl's point is almost the opposite. He argues we don't actually want a life without tension. We want a life *worth* the tension, something pulling us forward that's bigger than how we happen to feel on a given Tuesday.

The line I underlined and keep coming back to is his reworking of Nietzsche: *"He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how."* You can survive an astonishing amount of difficulty if the difficulty is *for* something. Take the why away, and even an easy life starts to feel unbearable.

What stayed with me most is that he flips the usual question. We tend to ask what we want from life: more of this, less of that. Frankl says life is really the one doing the asking, and our job is to answer it: with the work we take seriously, the people we show up for, the way we carry the things we can't change. Meaning isn't something you find lying around. It's something you're responsible for supplying.

I don't want to over-explain a book that says more in 150 pages than I can in a blog post. But if you've ever caught yourself chasing comfort and quietly wondering why it doesn't add up to much, this is the one to read. It reminded me that a sense of life, an actual reason and not just a routine, isn't a nice-to-have. It's the thing everything else hangs off.

Five stars. I'll be re-reading it.
